## Psychology and Focus
Reading psychology or spiritual literature forces us to wrestle with our view of the world.
These books often pose questions or offer viewpoints contrary to the views we hold when we read them.
The primary difference between reading these books and others is that these books often ask us to apply their concepts or change our behavior according to the considerations they offer.

For example, take "Surrounded by Idiots", by Thomas Erikson. 
This book talks about Erikson's analysis of personalities, specifically in the workplace.
Erikson categorizes people into four different personality groups, describes each archetype, and discusses their natural strengths and weaknesses.
The point of the book is that an ignorance of the differences in others' personalities can lead you to think less of them, when in reality, they have many strengths that you do not have.
Erikson's goal in writing this book is to enlighten his readers on personality types so that they can apply this to their working relationships to become better coworkers and increase group effectiveness.

Obviously, after reading that book, it would be on your mind the next day, or week, or however long, before becoming something you only think about ocassionally.
You could, of course, make reminders for yourself and, through intentionality, keep it at the forefront of your mind indefinitely, but you would need to continually make that the primary thought on your mind.

What would happen, then, if you switched to reading Scripture, which offered a different, non-contradictory way to view those same relationships?
Well, the human mind is like the eye: we can have a wide array of things in our peripherals, but we are only able to focus on one thing in any given instant.
You would then have to make the decision which paradigm to focus on.
You could, of course, switch between them rapidly in your mind, but that has its limits.

Furthermore, we are aware that there is more than just personalities to focus on in life.
There is our job, our family and friends, our faith, our responsibilities, and so much more.
At any given time, we are asked to make one thing or another the focus of our attention.

When we do this, we must release whatever new concept we have been trying to apply and focus on the present situation.

This human limitation has been on my mind recently, and it has brought me to a realization: our inability to focus on everything at once is the entire reason that we need habits.

## Gradually, Not All at Once
Think back to your time in school, specifically to a class you were uninterested in.
These classes were usually harder to pay attention in, and that resulted in not learning the content as well as you should have.
Coming up on an exam, the temptation was likely to cram the night before.
And while this may work to pass the exam, you would find a week later that you do not remember the content you crammed much at all.


Contrast that with classes that you loved.
In these classes, the lectures were engaging, and you made an effort to understand the content.
Coming up on an exam, you may still study last minute, but the continued, consistent exposure you had meant that you were brushing up on details, not learning concepts.
The exams felt easier because you actually knew the content; you weren't just regurgitating your study session from the previous night.

For one last example, consider learning to drive.
At the beginning, there were many moving pieces and the operations felt unnatural.
Watch the spedometer.
Remember which pedal is the brake.
Which way do I push the turn signal to go right?
Is there someone in the lane next to me?
Did the speed limit just drop?
Did I just miss my turn?

Imagine if you still had to manually think about each of those every time you drove.
Daily commutes would feel mentally taxing.
Trying to navigate downtown in a place you have never been would be next to impossible.

Thankfully, through repitition, our brain has made many parts of driving automatic.
When we are driving to work, we are often subconciously spacing ourself behind the car in front of us, not having to think about which pedal is which.
We just have to know to turn right, and our muscle memory knows exactly how much to slow the car down and when, and it also likely flips the turn signal.

All of these actions are necessary to driving, but they are so engrained that they do not have to be the center of our attention.

## Repitition
By now, you can probably guess where I am going with this: the habits we choose intentionally allow us to control what becomes automatic.

Driving only became automatic because of repitition.
Similarly, applying the concepts we learn from books and Scripture comes through repeated exposure to the material and repeated application.

This is why small daily habits are so effective.
You do not read Scripture every day to daily have a new revelation.
You read it so that your mind adopts the patterns in Scripture.

Like the path of a single raindrop becoming a river, daily exposure to God's word trains our thoughts to follow the same path as His.
Like picking up a pattern of speech from someone we are around often, daily exposure to God's word trains our speech to sound like His.
Like learning to driving a car, daily exposure to God's word trains our hands to act like His.

And after a while of these seemingly small habits, we look around us and see the massive change that has resulted.

---

It is tempting to try to learn everything in a day, trying to reach the finish line of understanding everything.
But God did not expect us to come out of the waters of baptism a perfect, righteous person, who never sinned again.
He expected and planned for us to need to grow.

It is through abiding in Him that He teaches us who He is and shapes who we are.
There is no level of understanding that He is waiting for us to get to before we are considered saved.
And there is no way that we could learn everything in Scripture in a day, much less have it meaningfully affect our character.

As God abides in us and we in Him, He teaches us how to be like Him.
He equips us for His will (2 Tim. 3:16, Heb. 13:20-21) through our experiences and teaches us through our circumstances.
We slowly learn who He is and who He wants us to be.

He has invited us on an incredibly journey, and He has already has all the details planned.
How incredible is it that, out of all the ways for God to choose for us to grow as people, He chose a daily relationship?
To be with us daily, and to one day be with us in Heaven forever.
Not rushed, but a walk.

And that is why our Christian walk is described as just that: a walk.
